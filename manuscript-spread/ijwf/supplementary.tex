\section{Predictors and flammability indices}
\label{sec:fi}

\subsection{The wind field}

Meteorological data with adequate spatial coverage do not exist for the region, and
the spread date of many fires is uncertain, so the wind was assumed not to change
during a fire. This is a coarse approximation, but fires in this region commonly
spread in a single dominant direction. The regional wind direction of each mapped
fire was taken from the ERA5 reanalysis \citep{hersbach2020era5} for its fortnight
and corrected manually where the fire's shape disagreed with it. From that direction
and a regional speed fixed at 14.4\,km\,h$^{-1}$ (4\,m\,s$^{-1}$) we generated 30\,m
fields of wind direction and speed with WindNinja \citep{forthofer2007windNinja},
using the mass-conservation solver and a 90\,m mesh, so that the field carries the
steering and acceleration of the wind by terrain. The regional speed is the same for
every fire; the fire-level wind coefficient $\beta_4$ (Eqn~1 of the main text)
absorbs between-fire variation in wind forcing.

\subsection{The Fire Weather Index}

Atmospheric conditions enter the model through the Fire Weather Index (FWI;
\citealt{van_wagner_development_1987}), taken from the ERA5-based daily product of
\citet{vitolo_era5-based_2020} at $\approx$\,24\,km resolution. The index varies
strongly along the west--east precipitation gradient, but our data cannot inform
that spatial variation, and the long-term spatial effects of climate are already
absorbed by the vegetation variables \citep{barbera_patterns}. We therefore
standardised the index per pixel, using the mean and standard deviation of its
1998--2023 daily series, so that the predictor represents climatic variation in time
and not in space. Standardised daily values were averaged by fortnight and
accumulated as a weighted mean over the focal fortnight and the 10 preceding ones,
with weights decaying into the past as $w_l = \exp[-(l/\tau)^2]$ (Eqn~2 of the main
text). The decay $\tau$ was not estimated with the spread model; it was fixed at
2.82 fortnights, the joint posterior mode of a model of fire size as a function of
accumulated FWI fitted beforehand to the mapped record.

\subsection{The flammability indices}

To parameterise the two flammability indices we modelled the probability that a
cell burned at least once over the period and study area of
\citet{barbera_patterns} (1999--2022, 24 fire seasons). The data were a random
sample of 9194 points taken in the burnable portion of the study area,
constrained to lie at least 800\,m apart. The model was

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:fimodel}
\begin{aligned}
    y_i &\sim \ \text{Bernoulli}(\theta_i) \\
    \text{logit}(\theta_i) &= a_{v[i]} + b_{v[i]} \, (\mathrm{NDVI}_i - c_{v[i]})
        + \delta_1 \, E_i + \delta_2 \, N_i + \delta_3 \, P_i \\[4pt]
    a_v &\sim \ \text{Normal}(\mu_a, \sigma_a) \\
    \log(-b_v) &\sim \ \text{Normal}(\mu_b, \sigma_b) \\
    \text{logit}(c_v) &\sim \ \text{Normal}(\mu_c, \sigma_c) \\[4pt]
    \mu_a &\sim \ \text{Normal}(0, 10), \qquad
    \sigma_a \sim \ \text{Normal}(0, 5)\text{T}[0, ] \\
    \mu_b &\sim \ \text{Normal}(\log(100), 2), \qquad
    \sigma_b \sim \ \text{Normal}(0, 2)\text{T}[0, ] \\
    \mu_c &\sim \ \text{Normal}(0, 5), \qquad
    \sigma_c \sim \ \text{Normal}(0, 3)\text{T}[0, ] \\[4pt]
    \delta_k &\sim \ \text{Normal}(0, 10), \qquad k \in \{1, 2, 3\},
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $y_i \in \{0, 1\}$ indicates whether cell $i$ burned over the
study period, $\theta_i$ is its burn probability, $E$ is elevation, $N$ is
northness weighted by slope (Eqn~\ref{eq:FI}) and $P$ is slope. The three
topographic variables were standardised for fitting and their coefficients
transformed back to the original scale afterwards. The subscript $v[i]$ is the
vegetation type of cell $i$, numbered as in Table~\ref{tab:vfi-params}. The
vegetation parameters $a$, $b$ and $c$ were given hierarchical priors so that
types with few data (dry forest above all) could be estimated; $b$ was
forced to be negative so that the effect of NDVI is unimodal, with $c_v$ the
NDVI at which burn probability peaks.

The slope coefficient $\delta_3$ is not part of the topographic flammability
index. It was included only to improve the estimation of the other parameters:
slope enters the spread model separately, and there it is \textit{directional},
since spread uphill and downhill differ. The linear predictor of
Eqn~\ref{eq:fimodel}, without the slope term, was split into its vegetation and
its topographic part, and each part was rescaled to mean 0 and standard
deviation 1 over the dataset used to fit this model:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:FI}
\begin{aligned}
    \mathrm{VFI} &= \{[a_v + b_v \, (\mathrm{NDVI} - c_v)] + 3.487888\} \ \frac{1}{1.225364} \\
    \mathrm{TFI} &= [(-0.002407865 \, E + 0.2937129 \, N) + 2.691936] \ \frac{1}{0.852505} \\
    N &= \cos \left(\Phi \, \frac{\pi}{180}\right) \frac{1}{1 + \exp(-5 + 35 P)},
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $a_v$, $b_v$ and $c_v$ vary by vegetation type $v$
(Table~\ref{tab:vfi-params}), $E$ is elevation (m\,a.s.l.), $\Phi$ is aspect
(degrees), $P$ is slope (degrees) and $N$ is northness weighted by slope, so
that it approaches zero on flat ground. Higher values mean more flammable
vegetation and more flammable topography. Fig.~\ref{fig:fi} shows the resulting
indices as functions of the variables that compose them.

\section{Parameter estimation}
\label{sec:estimation}

\subsection{Parameter bounds and priors}
\label{sec:priors}

Each spread parameter is bounded (Eqn~2 of the main text), so that the vector
$\boldsymbol{\beta}_{ft}$ follows a multivariate logit-normal distribution scaled
between the lower bounds $\mathbf{L}$ and the upper bounds $\mathbf{U}$,

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:limits}
\begin{aligned}
    \mathbf{L} &= \{-50, 0, 0, 0, 0, 2\} \\
    \mathbf{U} &= \{50, 30, 30, 158.6232, 30, \psi\}, \qquad
    \psi \sim \ \text{Uniform}(\psi_{\min}, 2000),
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\noindent in the order $\{\beta_0, \beta_1, \beta_2, \beta_3, \beta_4, \kappa\}$ of
Eqn~1 of the main text. The lower bound of zero on $\beta_1$ to $\beta_4$ imposes the
sign restrictions: flammability, uphill slope and a following wind can only increase
spread probability. The upper bound on the number of steps, $\psi$, is estimated. Its
prior upper limit of 2000 steps means a fire can travel at most
$2000 \times 30\,\mathrm{m} = 60\,\mathrm{km}$, which spans the west--east extent of the
Andean Patagonian forests in Argentina; larger fires would leave the study area, and we
have no data to inform spread probability in the steppe. Its lower limit $\psi_{\min}$
was set to the smallest value compatible with the fire-wise posteriors of stage 1
(Section~\ref{sec:stage2}). The predictor $x$ (accumulated FWI) was standardised before
fitting, and we used $\gamma_{0p} \sim \text{Normal}(0, 10)$ for
$p \in \{1, \ldots, 5\}$, $\gamma_{06} \sim \text{Normal}(-3.80, 10)$ and
$\boldsymbol{\gamma}_1 \sim \text{Normal}(0, 10)$, where $p$ indexes the six spread
parameters. The covariance matrix was decomposed into standard deviations and a
correlation matrix so that each could take its own prior:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:hier2}
\begin{aligned}
    \boldsymbol{\Sigma} &= \text{diag}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}) \, \boldsymbol{\Omega} \, \text{diag}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}) \\
    \sigma_p^2 &\sim \ \text{Inverse Gamma}(\alpha = 1, \beta = 0.001) \\
    \boldsymbol{\Omega} &= \text{diag}(\boldsymbol{V})^{-\frac{1}{2}} \, \boldsymbol{V} \, \text{diag}(\boldsymbol{V})^{-\frac{1}{2}} \\
    \boldsymbol{V} &\sim \ \text{Inverse Wishart}(\nu = 7, \boldsymbol{\Sigma} = \boldsymbol{I}),
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $\boldsymbol{V}$ is an auxiliary covariance matrix used only to place an
inverse-Wishart prior on the correlation matrix without its parameters constraining the
standard deviations; with $\nu = 7$ the implied prior on each correlation is
approximately uniform on $(-1, 1)$.

\subsection{Approximate Bayesian Computation}

The spread model can simulate fires but its likelihood cannot be evaluated, because the
cell-to-cell spread events $y_{ijk}$ (Eqn~1 of the main text) are not observed. At best
the ignition point and the final perimeter of a fire are known; how the fire moved from
cell to cell is not, and could not be recorded, since fire advances continuously in space
and time. We therefore used Approximate Bayesian Computation (ABC;
\citealt{beaumont2010approximate, sisson2018handbook}), as
\citet{morales_stochastic_2015} did for the same purpose. ABC applies when a model can
simulate data but its likelihood cannot be computed: parameter values are favoured in
proportion to how similar the data they simulate are to the observed ones. The ABC
posterior conditions on a summary of the data rather than on the data themselves,

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:abc}
p_{\mathrm{ABC}}(\vartheta \mid s_{\text{obs}}) \propto
\int K(||s_{\text{obs}} - s||) \ p(s \mid \vartheta) \ p(\vartheta) \ ds,
\end{equation}

\noindent where $s_{\text{obs}}$ summarises the observed data and $s$ the simulated ones,
$||\cdot||$ is a distance and $K(\cdot)$ a kernel mapping distances to (unnormalised)
densities, larger at smaller distances. Here $y_{\text{obs}}$ would be the unobserved
binary vector of spread events and $s_{\text{obs}}$ is the set of cells the fire burned.
Our dissimilarity is $||s_i - s_j|| = 1 - O_{ij}$, with $O_{ij}$ the spatial overlap
between the observed and a simulated fire,

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:overlap}
O_{ij} = \frac{|A_i \cap A_j|}{|A_i \cup A_j|},
\end{equation}

\noindent where $A$ is the set of cells burned by each fire, so that $O_{ij} = 1$ for
identical fires and 0 when they share no burned cell. Every simulation of a mapped fire
uses that fire's own data: its spatial predictors, its FWI and its ignition point.

The full hierarchical model could not be sampled directly: the parameters are many, the
kernel is stochastic, and it varies abruptly with $\boldsymbol{\beta}$, which makes
conventional MCMC prohibitively expensive. We therefore fitted it in two stages, following
\citet{lunn2013fully}: stage 1 samples the posterior of $\boldsymbol{\beta}_f$ separately
for each fire under a placeholder prior, and stage 2 combines those samples to estimate
the hyperparameters, discounting the placeholder prior in the process. Splitting the model
this way lets the difficult step, sampling $\boldsymbol{\beta}_f$, be handled with
whatever algorithm suits it, and it confines the fire simulator to stage 1: stage 2 never
runs it.

\subsection{Stage 1: fire-wise posteriors}

For each of the 57 fires with a known ignition point we sampled $\boldsymbol{\beta}_f$
with adaptive ABC sequential Monte Carlo \citep{delmoral2012adaptive}. The placeholder
prior is uniform over the bounded parameter space $[\mathbf{L}, \mathbf{U}]$
(Eqn~\ref{eq:limits}), which is a standard logistic distribution on the unconstrained scale on which
the sampler works. We used $N = 10\,000$ particles, initialised from a Sobol sequence, and
one simulated fire per particle. The tolerance $\varepsilon$ started at 1, the largest
possible dissimilarity, so that the first iteration keeps every particle; at each iteration
it was lowered by bisection to the value that halves the effective sample size, and was
never allowed to rise. Particles were reweighted under the kernel

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:abckernel}
K(d) = (1 - d) \ \mathbb{I}_{d \le \varepsilon},
\end{equation}

\noindent that is, the spatial overlap truncated at the current tolerance. Particles were
then resampled with probability proportional to their weights and moved by one Metropolis
step with a differential-evolution proposal \citep{terbraak2006markov}: the proposal for a
particle is its current position plus the difference between two other particles, drawn
with probability proportional to their weights, plus a Gaussian jitter of scale
$10^{-3}$; the scaling factor on the difference was 1. Each iteration therefore costs one
simulated fire per particle, 10\,000 in all, and this is where nearly all of the
computation of the fit is spent. Sampling stopped when the
acceptance rate fell below 0.015, when fewer than 300 distinct particles survived a move,
or when $\varepsilon$ stopped decreasing appreciably (less than $10^{-4}$ for ten
consecutive iterations).

The upper bound on $\kappa$ was set separately for each fire, by simulating that fire's
landscape with a spread probability of 1 and no step limit and recording the number of
steps consumed before no burnable cell remained within reach. Beyond that value more steps
cannot change the simulated fire, so the bound restricts the search without excluding any
attainable fire size.

\subsection{Stage 2: the hierarchical fit}
\label{sec:stage2}

Stage 2 estimated the hyperparameters ($\boldsymbol{\gamma}_0$, $\boldsymbol{\gamma}_1$,
$\boldsymbol{\sigma}$ and $\boldsymbol{\Omega}$; Eqn~2 of the main text and
Eqn~\ref{eq:hier2}), the random effects of the 57 fires with a known ignition point, the
number of steps of the 178 fires without one, the upper bound $\psi$ on the number of
steps (Eqn~\ref{eq:limits}) and the coefficients of an auxiliary regression, all jointly.

Every parameter is bounded, so the sampler works on transformed scales
(Eqn~\ref{eq:limits}). The five spread coefficients are sampled on the logit scale on
which they are modelled, scaled to their fixed bounds, which is also the scale of the
multivariate normal in Eqn~2 of the main text. The number of steps is different, because its upper bound $\psi$
is estimated rather than fixed: $\kappa_f$ is sampled on the simulator's own scale and
transformed to the scaled logit whenever the hierarchical density is evaluated, which
brings the Jacobian of that transform into every update that touches it. Stage 1 adds one
further complication. There, each fire's placeholder prior was defined on that fire's own
scale, bounded above by the largest number of steps its landscape can consume, whereas the
hierarchical model places all fires on the common scale $(2, \psi)$. The placeholder prior
is therefore evaluated on the fire-wise scale, with its own Jacobian, before being divided
out. The lower limit $\psi_{\min}$ of the prior on $\psi$ is the largest $\kappa$ drawn
anywhere in stage 1, that is, the smallest upper bound compatible with every fire-wise
posterior.

Stage 1 hands stage 2 nothing but samples: for each of the 57 fires, the 10\,000 particles
of its final SMC iteration, equally weighted after the last resampling step. Those clouds
are the proposal pools of the Lunn step below, and they are the reason stage 2 never runs
the fire simulator.

The auxiliary regression is what brings the 178 fires without an ignition point into the
fit. The 57 fires that have one are larger, on average, than the mapped record as a whole,
which would bias the parameters that govern simulated fire size: the number of steps
$\kappa_f$ and its hyperparameters $\gamma_{06}$, $\gamma_{16}$ and $\sigma_6$. To correct
that bias we related the estimated number of steps to fire size,

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:areasteps}
\begin{aligned}
    \log(a_f) &\sim \ \text{Normal}(\mu_f, \sigma_a)\text{T}[\log(10), ] \\
    \mu_f &= \alpha_0 + \alpha_1 \, \log(\kappa_f), \qquad
    \alpha_0 \sim \ \text{Normal}(-1.52, 10), \quad
    \alpha_1 \sim \ \text{Normal}(0, 10),
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\noindent with $a_f$ the area of fire $f$ (ha), truncated below at the 10\,ha mapping
threshold. The prior on $\alpha_0$ was centred on the mean of $\log(a)$ over all mapped
fires so as not to bias its estimation. A fire without an ignition point cannot be
simulated, so it has no stage-1 posterior; its $\kappa_f$ is inferred from its area
through this regression, and it informs the hyperparameters of $\kappa$ like any other
fire.

The sampler updates the following blocks in turn, so that each is drawn from its
conditional distribution given the current value of the others.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Hyperparameters of each spread parameter} ($\gamma_{0p}$, $\gamma_{1p}$ and
$\sigma_p^2$, for $p \in \{1, \ldots, 6\}$). Given the random effects, the hierarchical
mean (Eqn~2 of the main text) is an ordinary linear regression with normal errors on the
unconstrained scale, and its normal and inverse-gamma priors are conjugate
(Section~\ref{sec:priors}). The regression coefficients and the variance were therefore
drawn from their exact conditional distributions, which is a Gibbs step: conjugacy is
what makes the exact draw available, and drawing from the exact conditional is what
makes it Gibbs rather than Metropolis--Hastings.

\item \textbf{Correlation matrix} $\boldsymbol{\Omega}$. Drawn from its inverse-Wishart
conditional given the random-effect errors and then rescaled to a correlation matrix. Only
the correlation of that draw is used, which is what keeps the inverse-Wishart prior from
constraining the standard deviations (Eqn~\ref{eq:hier2}).

\item \textbf{Random effects of the 57 fires with a known ignition point}: the step
that makes the two-stage fit work \citep{lunn2013fully}. A candidate
$\boldsymbol{\beta}_f$ is drawn uniformly from that fire's 10\,000 stage-1 particles: an
independence proposal whose density is the fire-wise ABC posterior itself. That density cancels between
target and proposal, so the acceptance ratio reduces to the hierarchical density (Eqn~2 of
the main text) divided by the stage-1 placeholder prior, times the area likelihood
(Eqn~\ref{eq:areasteps}). Because $\kappa$ is sampled on the simulator's own scale but
modelled on a scaled logit whose upper bound is estimated, both densities carry the
Jacobian of that transform, and the placeholder prior is evaluated on the fire-wise scale
on which stage 1 defined it.

\item \textbf{Number of steps of the 178 fires without an ignition point.} These have no
stage-1 sample, so they were updated by random-walk Metropolis on the scaled-logit scale,
with the hierarchical density as prior and Eqn~\ref{eq:areasteps} as likelihood.

\item \textbf{Upper bound on the number of steps} $\psi$. Random-walk Metropolis on its
own scaled-logit scale, with the flat prior of Eqn~\ref{eq:limits}. Changing $\psi$
rescales the steps of every fire, so this update includes the area likelihood of the 178
fires and the Jacobian of the steps transform for the 57.

\item \textbf{Auxiliary regression} ($\alpha_0$, $\alpha_1$, $\sigma_a$). The truncated
normal likelihood is not conjugate, so these were updated by random-walk Metropolis.
\end{itemize}

The proposal standard deviations of the random-walk blocks were tuned in pilot runs, by
regressing the observed acceptance rate on the proposal scale and interpolating to an
acceptance rate of 0.44. The reported posterior comes from eight chains, started from
draws of a pilot run, each thinned to one draw in 1000 and each contributing 1500 draws
(12\,000 in total, from $1.5 \times 10^6$ iterations per chain); the eight chains ran in
parallel in about 15 hours. Convergence was satisfactory for every parameter, with
$\hat{R} \le 1.001$ and bulk and tail effective sample sizes above 10\,000.

\section{Validation}
\label{sec:validation}

The model was validated in the pattern-oriented sense
\citep{grimm2005pattern, hartig2011statistical}, by asking whether statistical patterns
emerging from many simulated fires match those of the observed record, using patterns the
fitting procedure did not target. Two exercises did this at two scales: one mapped fire at
a time, from its own ignition point, and the whole record at once. Neither recalibrated any
parameter.

\subsection{Why not classical cross-validation}

Holding out a fifth of the fires would have degraded the model without testing
what matters about it. Fifty-seven fires is already few for a six-parameter
hierarchical model with sign-constrained bounds, a multivariate logit-normal on
the transformed parameters and an auxiliary regression of area on the number of
steps that depends on the fitted $\kappa_f$; the observed size distribution is
heavy-tailed and sharply peaked, which makes it an unstable target to estimate
from a few dozen fires; and the hierarchical structure is itself a regulariser,
so a held-out fire's random effects would mostly reproduce the population mean
plus noise. The model is also deliberately underparameterised for the process
(fixed functional forms, sign restrictions, a fixed decay $\tau$, bounded
parameter ranges), so overfitting is not the failure mode cross-validation is
designed to catch. What we want to know is whether the simulator generates fires
with the statistical properties of the ones that actually occur, which is the
question pattern-oriented validation asks, and it is the standard by which
burn-probability models are usually judged \citep{parisien2020commentary}.

\subsection{Re-simulating the mapped fires}

For each of the 57 fires with a known ignition point we simulated 2000 fires from that
fire's ignition cell on that fire's landscape, twice: once under the fire's own fitted
random effects and once under random effects drawn afresh from the population distribution
at that fire's FWI (Eqn~2 of the main text). Each simulated fire was reduced to its spatial
overlap with the observed one (Eqn~\ref{eq:overlap}), its burned area overall and within
each of the five vegetation classes, its compactness ($4\pi \times$ area / perimeter$^2$)
and the deviation of its principal axis from the fire's wind axis.

Orientation is the bearing of the leading eigenvector of a principal component analysis of
the coordinates of the burned cells. The wind axis of a fire is the circular mean of wind
direction over the cells the \emph{observed} fire burned, and the same axis scores that
fire and all of its simulations, since WindNinja steers the wind field by terrain and one
regional direction does not describe every fire.

Each statistic was then turned into a scaled quantile residual
\citep{dunn1996randomized}, computed with \texttt{DHARMa} v0.4.7 \citep{DHARMa}: the
position of the observed value within the 2000 simulated ones, randomised across ties for
the discrete areas, which is uniform on $(0, 1)$ if the model is calibrated for that
statistic. Sorting the 57 residuals against the expected uniform quantiles gives one
quantile--quantile plot per statistic; points below the 1:1 line mean the model simulates
too much of the quantity, points above it too little.

Two cases were treated separately. A vegetation class with fewer than 30 burnable cells
(2.7\,ha) in a fire's landscape was dropped from that class's panel: with almost nothing of
the class available to burn, observed and simulated areas are both zero and the residual is
only tie randomisation. Simulations that burned fewer than three cells have no principal
axis, and were resampled from the same fire's remaining draws, so the two shape statistics
ask a conditional question: given that the model produced a fire at all, is the observed
shape a typical one?

\subsection{The regional simulation experiment}

We also simulated fires over the whole study area, partitioned into four latitudinal tiles
that together cover 99.1\,\% of it, the domain over which the observed record is complete.
For each simulated fire we drew, in this order:

\begin{itemize}
\item an FWI value, resampled with replacement from the values of the mapped fires, so the
simulated marginal matches the observed one exactly;

\item a draw from the joint posterior of the hyperparameters;

\item a vector of random effects for a \emph{new} fire from the population distribution
$\text{MVLN}(\boldsymbol{\mu}(\mathrm{FWI}), \boldsymbol{\Sigma})$, so that both
hyperparameter and between-fire uncertainty are propagated and no fitted fire's own random
effects are reused;

\item a tile, with probability proportional to the number of eligible cells it contains;

\item and an ignition cell, uniformly among those.
\end{itemize}

Eligible cells are those inside the study area, burnable, and far enough from the tile
borders that a fire of $\kappa$ steps cannot reach them. Since the automaton advances at
most one cell per step, this guarantees that no fire is truncated by a tile border. Drawing
$\kappa$ before the tile and the ignition cell, rather than rejecting ignitions that do not
fit, leaves the marginal distribution of $\kappa$ untouched and so does not shrink the
simulated size distribution.

Simulated fires below 10\,ha were discarded, because the mapping records nothing smaller
and they have no observed counterpart. One pass of 148\,649 proposals left 64\,836 fires of
at least 10\,ha (43.6\,\%), and those are the simulated fires the paper analyses.

\subsection{Comparing simulated with observed fires}

Simulated and observed fires were compared in two ways.

\textbf{Size distribution.} Simulated against observed $\log_{10}(\mathrm{area})$, as
overlaid densities, a quantile--quantile plot and a Kolmogorov--Smirnov statistic.

\textbf{Shape.} From the burned cells of each fire in raster space we computed area,
perimeter, compactness and orientation, the last reported as its deviation from the
dominant wind axis: $0^\circ$ for a fire running along the wind, $90^\circ$ across it, and
$45^\circ$ the expectation if fires were oriented at random. Here the axis is the fixed
regional direction the tiles were driven with (113/293$^\circ$), because the observed fires
without a mapped ignition point carry no wind field of their own. These metrics need only a
rasterised perimeter, so the observed reference is every mapped fire in the record, across
every size class, and not only the 57 with a known ignition point.

Both shape statistics depend strongly on fire size, and size is itself one of the things
being tested, so the comparisons are made conditionally on burned area as well as
marginally. We repeated them conditionally on FWI, which tests the model's most distinctive
structural claim: that the parameters of spread shift with fire weather.

\section{The effect of vegetation type on spread probability as a function of
fire weather}
\label{sec:vegeffect}

One question the spread model is meant to answer is how the effect of
vegetation type on spread probability changes with fire weather. The
coefficient on VFI ($\beta_1$, Eqn~1 of the main text) did not vary with FWI
(Fig.~3 of the main text), but the nonlinearity of the logistic function means
that the effect of one variable on the probability depends on the values and
effects of the others, even with no interaction terms in the linear predictor.
This matters here because FWI does affect other coefficients, notably those
governing wind and slope. If a rising wind coefficient pushes spread
probability close to 1, the effect of vegetation should be attenuated, at least
for spread in the downwind direction. To evaluate this implicit interaction we
computed how the effect of vegetation type on spread probability changes with
FWI, propagating the effect of FWI on all six spread parameters.

To compare vegetation types we fixed elevation at 900\,m\,a.s.l.\ and aspect at
$90^\circ$ (northness $= 0$), which fixes TFI. To retain the directional effects
of slope and wind, and their joint variation over the landscape, we sampled 500
cells of the burnable area of Nahuel Huapi National Park, in the centre of the
study area, from the same raster used for simulation, which carries wind speed
and slope among other layers. To weigh uphill and downhill spread equally we
duplicated the set of cells and set slope to $0^\circ$ in the second half: the
spread model assumes that downhill spread is like spread on flat ground
(Eqn~1 of the main text).

Comparing vegetation types also requires a VFI for each, and therefore an NDVI.
Because NDVI varies with topography, we predicted its expected value for the
topographic conditions analysed (the 500 sampled slopes, with elevation and
aspect fixed) with a generalised additive model with a Beta distribution for
NDVI rescaled to $[0, 1]$, fitted to the same dataset used for the flammability
indices (Section~\ref{sec:fi}) and with slope, elevation and aspect as
predictors. From this we obtained the expected NDVI at 900\,m\,a.s.l.\ on
east-facing slopes over all sampled slope values, and from it the VFI of each
vegetation type in every sampled cell.

With elevation and aspect fixed, and with the 500 sampled values of slope and
wind speed (plus the 500 with slope set to zero) and their VFI for each
vegetation type, we computed spread probability by vegetation type along a
sequence of FWI values, averaging the predictions over the sampled cells. The
predictions therefore condition on fixed values of elevation, aspect and
vegetation type and marginalise over the distribution of wind speed, slope and
NDVI in the landscape, always assuming that the wind blows in the direction of
spread. They also marginalise over the random effects, by simulation. The
effect of vegetation type (Fig.~4A of the main text) is the mean absolute
deviation across the five types, all weighted equally.

\subsection{Correlation among the spread parameters}

Allowing the parameters to vary among fires also lets them covary.
Fig.~\ref{fig:corr} shows the posterior of the Pearson correlation between each
pair of parameters, computed on the scale the simulator uses rather than on the
unconstrained scale the multivariate normal is defined on, by simulating a full
set of fires from each posterior draw and back-transforming. Two distributions
are shown per pair. The one conditional on FWI fixes FWI at the mean of the
spread data and so represents the posterior of $\boldsymbol{\Omega}$
(Eqn~\ref{eq:hier2}); the marginal one lets each fire take its own FWI, and therefore
also carries any association induced by two parameters responding to fire
weather in the same, or in the opposite, direction. Where the two coincide, FWI
explains none of the association between that pair.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figS3_parameter_correlations}

each pair of spread parameters, marginal to or conditional on FWI. Coefficients
were computed by simulating parameter vectors from the fitted multivariate
distribution. The distributions conditional on FWI fix it at the mean of the

\subsection{Fit to the mapped fires}

The two figures below describe the fit of the spread model to the 57 fires with
a known ignition point. As described in the main text, a parameter vector
$\boldsymbol{\beta}$ was estimated for each fire. Letting these parameters vary
among fires is how the model represents unobserved variables that matter for
fire: a fire put out quickly by crews or by rain may have a smaller number of
steps ($\kappa$) than average, and a fire that spread under a strong wind may
have a large wind coefficient ($\beta_4$). From the variation in
$\boldsymbol{\beta}$ among fires, and from how that variation relates to each
fire's FWI, we estimated a distribution of parameters (Eqn~2 of the main text),
which is what lets new $\boldsymbol{\beta}$ be simulated for hypothetical fires.

Model fit can therefore be assessed in two ways: with the random effects
\textit{fitted} to each fire, or with new ones \textit{simulated} from the
estimated distribution. Simulated fires are expected to resemble the observed
ones more closely in the first case. In the second we are simulating fires that
differ from the observed ones precisely in the unobserved variables those
random effects absorb, such as wind speed, suppression and rainfall. The
comparison is therefore not a test of the deterministic part of the model but a
measurement of how wide that distribution is. Each fire was simulated 2000 times
in each mode.

\includegraphics[width=0.5\textwidth]{figS4_overlap}

ignition point; points and lines are the mean and the 95\,\% credible interval

Although the model fits poorly when the random effects are simulated, our aim was not a
simulator that predicts the advance of a particular fire, but one able to
generate fires similar to those that occur in the study area. The fit obtained
with simulated random effects is therefore not by itself a reason to reject the
model. Indeed, despite having been estimated with limited or no information on
atmospheric conditions and fire suppression, the model produces fires whose
sizes are of the same order as the observed ones (Fig.~\ref{fig:quotient}B).
Where it does fail, in fire shape, the failure is structural, and is discussed
in the main text.
