\subsection{Study area}

The study area covers 29\,214\,km$^2$ of the Subantarctic and High-Andean
phytogeographic provinces of Argentina \citep{oyarzabal_vegetation_2018}, between
39.0$^\circ$ and 44.5$^\circ$\,S: a strip roughly 600\,km from north to south and
50\,km from west to east along the eastern slope of the Andes. It contains
almost all of the continuous forests and shrublands of northwestern Andean
Patagonia (Fig.~\ref{fig:study-area}).
Precipitation falls steeply from west to east, from 3000--4000\,mm\,year$^{-1}$ to
about 500\,mm\,year$^{-1}$ over a few tens of kilometres \citep{villalba_large-scale_2003},
with wet, snowy winters and dry summers. Elevation ranges from 200 to 3700\,m\,a.s.l.,
but burnable vegetation is confined below $\approx$\,1750\,m\,a.s.l., where mean annual
temperature ranges from about 0 to 13\,$^\circ$C. Lakes, glaciers, unvegetated
high-Andean ground and urban areas make up about a quarter of the area and are treated
as non-burnable.

Vegetation follows the precipitation gradient, elevation and local topography.
Wet forests are dominated by the evergreen \textit{Nothofagus dombeyi} (Mirb.) Oerst.
with an understorey of the bamboo \textit{Chusquea culeou} E. Desv.; subalpine forests
of the deciduous \textit{Nothofagus pumilio} (Poepp. \& Endl.) Krasser occupy steep
slopes from $\approx$\,1200\,m\,a.s.l. to treeline; dry forests of the conifer
\textit{Austrocedrus chilensis} (D. Don) Pic.Serm. \& Bizzarri occupy low elevations at
intermediate precipitation; shrublands are formed by resprouting tall shrubs and low
trees such as \textit{Nothofagus antarctica} (G. Forst.) Oerst., \textit{Lomatia
hirsuta} (Lam.) Diels ex J.F. Macbr. and \textit{Schinus patagonicus} (Phil.) I.M.
Johnst. ex Cabrera, often with abundant \textit{C. culeou}; and grasslands correspond
mostly to the Patagonian steppe, with tussock grasses of \textit{Pappostipa} and
\textit{Festuca} and the cushion shrub \textit{Mulinum spinosum} (Cav.) Pers. Fuel
amount and continuity differ sharply among these types: tall forests carry a sparse,
moist understorey vertically separated from the canopy, whereas shrublands have
vertically continuous fuels and a higher proportion of dry fine dead fuel
\citep{paritsis_positive_2015, tiribelli_changes_2018, barbera_microclimate_2023}.

Fires occur in late spring and summer (October--March), when fuels are dry enough, and
are ignited both by lightning and by people \citep{kitzberger_climatic_1997}. The most
fire-prone years are those with warm summers preceded by dry springs
\citep{kitzberger_climatic_1997, veblen_fire_2003}.

\subsection{Data}

Fires were taken from the atlas of \citet{barbera_patterns}, which maps every fire
$\ge$\,10\,ha in the study area over the 24 fire seasons from July 1998 to June 2022
from Landsat imagery at 30\,m resolution. Weather data were available for 235 of them, and
those are the fires used here. Fitting the spread model requires the ignition point as well
as the final perimeter, and the ignition point could be established for 57 fires, supplied
by fire management personnel and by a lightning-ignition database
(Fig.~\ref{fig:study-area}A).
The remaining 178 fires were used to inform the parameters that control simulated fire
size, as described below.

The model uses five groups of predictors, all resolved to the 30\,m grid:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Vegetation type}, in five classes (wet forest, subalpine forest, dry
forest, shrubland, grassland), reclassified from the regional forest and land-cover map
of \citet{mapa_ciefap}; areas burned before that map was produced were patched with the
map of \citet{lara_vegetacion_1999}.

\item \textbf{NDVI} (Normalized Difference Vegetation Index), averaged over all
available Landsat images from December to March of the summer preceding each fire
season, as a proxy for productivity and fuel load.

\item \textbf{Elevation, slope and aspect}, derived from the 30\,m SRTM V3 digital
elevation model \citep{DEMfarr2007}. From aspect we computed \textit{northness} as its
cosine, which is 1 on north-facing and $-1$ on south-facing slopes.

\item \textbf{Wind direction and speed}. Lacking meteorological data with adequate
spatial coverage, and with uncertain spread dates for many fires, we assumed that wind
does not change during a fire. This is a coarse approximation, but fires in this region
commonly spread in a single dominant direction. From a regional direction we generated
30\,m direction and speed fields with WindNinja \citep{forthofer2007windNinja}, using
the mass-conservation solver, a 90\,m mesh and a regional speed fixed at
14.4\,km\,h$^{-1}$. The regional direction of each mapped fire was taken from the ERA5
reanalysis \citep{hersbach2020era5} and corrected manually where the fire's shape
disagreed with it. The regional speed is held constant, but the model estimates a
fire-level coefficient that absorbs between-fire variation in wind forcing.

\item \textbf{Atmospheric conditions}, summarized by the Fire Weather Index
(FWI; \citealt{van_wagner_development_1987}), the single weather variable in the model
because it integrates relative humidity, temperature, precipitation and wind speed and
so keeps the number of parameters low. We used the ERA5-based daily FWI of
\citet{vitolo_era5-based_2020} at $\approx$\,24\,km resolution. Because the index varies
strongly along the precipitation gradient while our data cannot inform that variation,
we standardized it per pixel using the mean and standard deviation of the 1998--2023
series, so the predictor represents climatic variation in time and not in space; the
long-term spatial effects of climate are absorbed by the vegetation variables
\citep{barbera_patterns}. Standardized daily values were averaged by fortnight and then
accumulated as a weighted mean over the focal fortnight and the 10 preceding ones, with
weights decaying into the past as $w_l = \exp[-(l/\tau)^2]$. The decay $\tau$ was fixed
at 2.82 fortnights, the joint posterior mode of a model of fire size as a function of
accumulated FWI.
\end{itemize}

Space and time are discrete throughout: the landscape is a rectangular grid of
30\,m\,$\times$\,30\,m cells and the calendar is divided into 14-day fortnights, with
years running from 1 July to 30 June and named after the month in which they end.

\subsection{The spread model}

\subsubsection*{Flammability indices}

Representing the effects of vegetation type, productivity and topography on
flammability with one coefficient each would have required more parameters than the
data can support. We therefore summarized those predictors into two indices: a
\textbf{vegetation flammability index} (VFI) and a \textbf{topographic flammability
index} (TFI). Rather than an ordination, we used the linear predictor of a logistic
regression of the probability that a cell burned at least once over the study period,
fitted at the regional scale to a sample of 9194 points at least 800\,m apart
\citep{barbera_patterns}, and split it into a vegetation and a topographic part:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:FI}
\begin{aligned}
    \mathrm{VFI} &= \{[a_v + b_v \, (\mathrm{NDVI} - c_v)] + 3.487888\} \ \frac{1}{1.225364} \\
    \mathrm{TFI} &= [(-0.002407865 \, E + 0.2937129 \, N) + 2.691936] \ \frac{1}{0.852505} \\
    N &= \cos \left(\Phi \, \frac{\pi}{180}\right) \frac{1}{1 + \exp(-5 + 35 P)},
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $a_v$, $b_v$ and $c_v$ vary by vegetation type $v$ (Table~1), $E$ is
elevation (m\,a.s.l.), $\Phi$ is aspect (degrees), $P$ is slope (degrees) and $N$ is
northness weighted by slope, so that it approaches zero on flat ground. Both indices are
scaled to mean 0 and standard deviation 1 over the study area. Higher values mean more
flammable vegetation and more flammable topography: VFI is highest in shrublands and dry
forests and lowest in wet forests, and TFI decreases with elevation and increases towards
north-facing slopes.

\subsubsection*{The cellular automaton}

The spread model propagates fire from burning cells to their eight neighbours, with a
structure close to that of \citet{morales_stochastic_2015}. Cells are in one of  % TODO cite the model-schematic figure here if one is included
three states --- burning, burnable or non-burnable --- and a burning cell stays burning
for exactly one iteration (\textit{step} from here on), after which it becomes
non-burnable. Cells may be non-burnable from the start (water, unvegetated ground) or
because they burned earlier in the same year. Simulation starts from at least one
burning cell; at each step, spread from every burning cell to each of its burnable
neighbours is drawn as a Bernoulli trial, and the step ends once all burning cells have
been resolved. A cell with several burning neighbours may be reached by any of them, and
the order in which they are tried is immaterial. The simulation stops when the maximum
number of steps is reached, or earlier if no burning cell manages to spread. Fires are
assumed to occur entirely within one fortnight, so the model is atemporal within that
period.

Spread $y \in \{0, 1\}$ from cell $i$ to a burnable neighbour $j$ at step $k$ is

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:datamodel}
\begin{aligned}
    y_{ijk} &\sim \ \text{Bernoulli}(\theta_{ijk}) \\
    \theta_{ijk} &= \frac{\mathbb{I}_{k \le \kappa}}{1 + \exp{(-\eta_{ij})}} \\
    \eta_{ij} &= \beta_0 + \beta_1 \, \mathrm{VFI}_j + \beta_2 \, \mathrm{TFI}_j
                 + \beta_3 \, S_{ij} + \beta_4 \, W_{ij} \\
    S_{ij} &= \sin\left[ \tan^{-1} \left( \frac{E_j - E_i}{G_{ij}} \right) \right]
              \mathbb{I}_{E_j - E_i > 0} \\
    W_{ij} &= \cos(A_{ij} - D_i) \, V_i,
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $\mathbb{I}_{k \le \kappa}$ indicates whether the number of steps
$\kappa$ has been exceeded. VFI and TFI are properties of the receiving cell, whereas
$S_{ij}$ and $W_{ij}$ are \textit{directional} terms computed from the donor--receiver
pair: $E$ is elevation (m), $G_{ij}$ the distance between cell centres (m), $A_{ij}$ the
direction from which fire would arrive --- the position of the donor as seen from the
receiver (radians) --- $D_i$ the direction the wind blows from at the donor (radians) and
$V_i$ the wind speed at the donor (m\,s$^{-1}$). The first factor of $S_{ij}$ is the sine
of the slope between the two cells, equal to 1 if the receiver lies directly above the
donor and $-1$ if directly below; because cells do not overlap it rarely exceeds
$\sin(45^\circ) = 0.7071$. The indicator $\mathbb{I}_{E_j - E_i > 0}$ switches the term
off for downhill spread. The first factor of $W_{ij}$ is 1 when the wind blows exactly
along the direction of spread, $-1$ when exactly against it and 0 when perpendicular,
and $V_i$ gives the term its magnitude. Over the eight-cell neighbourhood the distances
$G_{ij}$ and directions $A_{ij}$ (in degrees) are

\[
    G =
    \begin{bmatrix}
        30 \sqrt{2} & 30 & 30 \sqrt{2} \\
        30 & \cdot & 30 \\
        30 \sqrt{2} & 30 & 30 \sqrt{2}
    \end{bmatrix}, \quad
    A =
    \begin{bmatrix}
        135 & 180 & 225 \\
        90 & \cdot & 270 \\
        45 & 0 & 315
    \end{bmatrix},
\]

\noindent with $\cdot$ marking the donor. Distance to roads and settlements is assumed
not to affect spread probability. The unknowns are the five coefficients and the number
of steps, collected as
$\boldsymbol{\beta} = \{\beta_0, \beta_1, \beta_2, \beta_3, \beta_4, \kappa\}$.

\subsubsection*{Hierarchical structure}

One of our aims was to model the effect of weather on spread. Weather station density in
the region is far too low to give adequate spatial and temporal resolution over decades,
so we used the coarse, pixel-standardized FWI described above, assumed it constant within
a fire, and took its value at the ignition cell. Even with that variation, the model
remains a simple representation of spread: wind direction and regional speed are constant
within a fire, and suppression is not represented at all. To absorb the process variance
the model does not describe, we gave the parameters themselves a statistical model ---
that is, a hierarchical (multilevel) structure --- so that FWI sets the \emph{mean} of a
distribution of spread parameters rather than determining them. Two fires with the same
FWI can then differ in weather, suppression, soil moisture and terrain particulars:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:hier1}
\begin{aligned}
    \boldsymbol{\beta}_{ft} &= \mathbf{L} + \frac{\mathbf{U} - \mathbf{L}}{1 + \exp{(\boldsymbol{\eta}_{ft}})} \\
    \boldsymbol{\eta}_{ft} &\sim \ \text{Multivariate Normal}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{ft}, \boldsymbol{\Sigma}) \\
    \boldsymbol{\mu}_{ft} &= \boldsymbol{\gamma}_0 + \boldsymbol{\gamma}_1 \, x_{ft} \\
    x_{ft} &= \sum_{l=0}^{10} \mathrm{FWI}_{f,t-l} \ \frac{w_l}{\sum_{j=0}^{10} w_j},
    \qquad w_l = e^{-\left(\frac{l}{\tau}\right)^2},
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $\boldsymbol{\beta}_{ft}$ is the parameter vector of fire $f$, which
occurred in fortnight $t$, and $\mathbf{L}$ and $\mathbf{U}$ hold the lower and upper
bound of each parameter. Bounding the parameters simplified estimation and made sign
restrictions easy to impose, so that $\boldsymbol{\beta}_{ft}$ follows a multivariate
logit-normal distribution scaled between $\mathbf{L}$ and $\mathbf{U}$. The bounds were

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:limits}
\begin{aligned}
    \mathbf{L} &= \{-50, 0, 0, 0, 0, 2\} \\
    \mathbf{U} &= \{50, 30, 30, 158.6232, 30, \psi\}, \qquad
    \psi \sim \ \text{Uniform}(\psi_{\min}, 2000),
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\noindent the upper bound on the number of steps, $\psi$, being estimated. Its prior
upper limit of 2000 steps means a fire can travel at most
$2000 \times 30\,\mathrm{m} = 60\,\mathrm{km}$, which spans the west--east extent of the
Andean Patagonian forests in Argentina; larger fires would leave the study area, and we
have no data to inform spread probability in the steppe. Its lower limit $\psi_{\min}$
was set to the smallest value compatible with the fire-wise posteriors of stage 1
(below). The predictor $x$ was standardized before fitting, and we used
$\gamma_{0p} \sim \text{Normal}(0, 10)$ for $p \in \{1, \ldots, 5\}$,
$\gamma_{06} \sim \text{Normal}(-3.80, 10)$ and
$\boldsymbol{\gamma}_1 \sim \text{Normal}(0, 10)$, where $p$ indexes the six spread
parameters. The covariance matrix was decomposed into standard deviations and a
correlation matrix so that each could take its own prior:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:hier2}
\begin{aligned}
    \boldsymbol{\Sigma} &= \text{diag}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}) \, \boldsymbol{\Omega} \, \text{diag}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}) \\
    \sigma_p^2 &\sim \ \text{Inverse Gamma}(\alpha = 1, \beta = 0.001) \\
    \boldsymbol{\Omega} &= \text{diag}(\boldsymbol{V})^{-\frac{1}{2}} \, \boldsymbol{V} \, \text{diag}(\boldsymbol{V})^{-\frac{1}{2}} \\
    \boldsymbol{V} &\sim \ \text{Inverse Wishart}(\nu = 7, \boldsymbol{\Sigma} = \boldsymbol{I}),
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $\boldsymbol{V}$ is an auxiliary covariance matrix used only to place an
inverse-Wishart prior on the correlation matrix without its parameters constraining the
standard deviations; with $\nu = 7$ the implied prior on each correlation is
approximately uniform on $(-1, 1)$.

Because the parameters have a distribution, a mapped fire can be simulated either with a
parameter vector drawn from that distribution given its FWI, or with the vector estimated
for that particular fire. We call these \textit{simulated} and \textit{fitted} parameters
respectively.

\subsection{Parameter estimation}

Fitting the spread model is hard because the cell-to-cell spread events $y_{ijk}$ are not
observed. At best the ignition point and the final perimeter of a fire are known; how the
fire moved from cell to cell is not, and could not be recorded, since fire advances
continuously in space and time. Without those data the likelihood cannot be evaluated, so
we used Approximate Bayesian Computation (ABC; \citealt{beaumont2010approximate,
sisson2018handbook}), as \citet{morales_stochastic_2015} did for the same purpose. ABC
applies when a model can simulate data but its likelihood cannot be computed: parameter
values are favoured in proportion to how similar the data they simulate are to the
observed ones. The ABC posterior conditions on a summary of the data rather than on the
data themselves,

\[
p_{\mathrm{ABC}}(\vartheta \mid s_{\text{obs}}) \propto
\int K(||s_{\text{obs}} - s||) \ p(s \mid \vartheta) \ p(\vartheta) \ ds,
\]

\noindent where $s_{\text{obs}}$ summarizes the observed data and $s$ the simulated ones,
$||\cdot||$ is a distance and $K(\cdot)$ a kernel mapping distances to (unnormalized)
densities, larger at smaller distances. Here $y_{\text{obs}}$ would be the unobserved
binary vector of spread events and $s_{\text{obs}}$ is the set of cells the fire burned.
Our dissimilarity is $||s_i - s_j|| = 1 - O_{ij}$, with $O_{ij}$ the spatial overlap
between the observed and a simulated fire,

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:overlap}
O_{ij} = \frac{|A_i \cap A_j|}{|A_i \cup A_j|},
\end{equation}

\noindent where $A$ is the set of cells burned by each fire, so that $O_{ij} = 1$ for
identical fires and 0 when they share no burned cell. Every simulation of a mapped fire
uses that fire's own data: its spatial predictors, its FWI and its ignition point.

The full hierarchical model could not be sampled directly: the parameters are many, the
kernel is stochastic, and it varies abruptly with $\boldsymbol{\beta}$, which makes
conventional MCMC prohibitively expensive. We therefore fitted it in two stages, following
\citet{lunn2013fully}: stage 1 samples the posterior of $\boldsymbol{\beta}_f$ separately
for each fire under a placeholder prior, and stage 2 combines those samples to estimate the
hyperparameters, discounting the placeholder prior in the process. Splitting the model this
way lets the difficult step --- sampling $\boldsymbol{\beta}_f$ --- be handled with
whatever algorithm suits it.

\subsubsection*{Stage 1 --- fire-wise posteriors}

For each of the 57 fires with a known ignition point we sampled $\boldsymbol{\beta}_f$
with adaptive ABC sequential Monte Carlo \citep{delmoral2012adaptive}. The placeholder prior
is uniform over the bounded parameter space $[\mathbf{L}, \mathbf{U}]$, which is a
standard logistic distribution on the unconstrained scale on which the sampler works. We
used $N = 10\,000$ particles, initialized from a Sobol sequence, and one simulated fire
per particle. At each iteration the tolerance $\varepsilon$ was lowered by bisection to
the value that halves the effective sample size, particles were reweighted under the
kernel

\[
K(d) = (1 - d) \ \mathbb{I}_{d \le \varepsilon},
\]

\noindent that is, the spatial overlap truncated at the current tolerance, and then moved
by one Metropolis step with a differential-evolution proposal
\citep{terbraak2006markov}: the proposal for a particle is its current position plus the
scaled difference between two other particles drawn with probability proportional to
their weights, plus a small Gaussian jitter (scale $10^{-3}$). Sampling stopped when the
acceptance rate fell below 0.015, when fewer than 300 distinct particles survived a move,
or when $\varepsilon$ stopped decreasing appreciably (less than $10^{-4}$ for ten
consecutive iterations). The upper bound on $\kappa$ was set separately for each fire, by
simulating that fire's landscape with a spread probability of 1 and no step limit and
recording the number of steps consumed before no burnable cell remained within reach.
Beyond that value more steps cannot change the simulated fire, so the bound restricts the
search without excluding any attainable fire size.

\subsubsection*{Stage 2 --- hierarchical fit}

Stage 2 ran an MCMC over all parameters, using the stage-1 samples as the proposal
distribution for each $\boldsymbol{\beta}_f$, discounting the stage-1 placeholder prior, and
updating most hyperparameters by Gibbs steps \citep{lunn2013fully}.

The 57 fires with a known ignition point are larger, on average, than the mapped record as
a whole, which would bias the parameters that govern simulated fire size --- the number of
steps $\kappa_f$ and its hyperparameters $\gamma_{06}$, $\gamma_{16}$ and $\sigma_6$. To
correct that bias we fitted, jointly with the rest of the model, an auxiliary regression
relating the estimated number of steps to fire size, which lets the steps of the 178 fires
with unknown ignition point --- which cannot be simulated --- be inferred from their area:

\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    \log(a_f) &\sim \ \text{Normal}(\mu_f, \sigma_a)\text{T}[\log(10), ] \\
    \mu_f &= \alpha_0 + \alpha_1 \, \log(\kappa_f), \qquad
    \alpha_0 \sim \ \text{Normal}(-1.52, 10), \quad
    \alpha_1 \sim \ \text{Normal}(0, 10),
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\noindent with $a_f$ the area of fire $f$ (ha), truncated below at the 10\,ha mapping
threshold. The prior on $\alpha_0$ was centred on the mean of $\log(a)$ over all mapped
fires so as not to bias its estimation.

The fire simulator is written in C\texttt{++} and distributed as the R package
\texttt{FireSpread} v1.4 \citep{firespread} through \texttt{Rcpp} v1.1.0 \citep{Rcpp}. All
fitting and analysis was done in R 4.5.3 \citep{R}, with \texttt{terra} v1.8.70
\citep{terra} for spatial data.

\subsection{Validation}

Classical train/test validation is not appropriate here, for reasons we return to in the
Discussion, and burn-probability models of this kind are conventionally judged on the
emergent properties of the regime they generate rather than on point predictions of
individual fires \citep{parisien2020commentary}. We therefore validated the model in the
pattern-oriented sense \citep{grimm2005pattern, hartig2011statistical}: we asked whether
statistical patterns emerging from many simulated fires match those of the observed
record, using patterns the fitting procedure did not target. Two exercises did this at
two scales --- one mapped fire at a time, from its own ignition point, and the whole
record at once --- and neither recalibrated any parameter.

\subsubsection*{Re-simulating the mapped fires}

For each of the 57 fires with a known ignition point we simulated 2000 fires from that
fire's ignition cell on that fire's landscape, twice: once under the fire's own fitted
parameter vector and once under a vector drawn afresh from the population distribution at
that fire's FWI --- the fitted and the simulated parameters defined above. Each simulated
fire was reduced to its spatial overlap with the observed one (Eqn~\ref{eq:overlap}), its
burned area overall and within each of the five vegetation classes, its compactness
($4\pi \times$ area / perimeter$^2$) and the deviation of its principal axis from the
fire's wind axis. Orientation is the bearing of the leading eigenvector of a principal
component analysis of the coordinates of the burned cells; the wind axis of a fire is the
circular mean of wind direction over the cells the \emph{observed} fire burned, and the
same axis scores that fire and all of its simulations, since WindNinja steers the field
by terrain and one regional direction does not describe every fire.

Each statistic was then turned into a scaled quantile residual
(\texttt{DHARMa} v0.4.7; \citealt{DHARMa}): the position of the observed value within the
2000 simulated ones, randomized across ties for the discrete areas, which is uniform on
$(0, 1)$ if the model is calibrated for that statistic. Sorting the 57 residuals against
the expected uniform quantiles gives one quantile--quantile plot per statistic; points
below the 1:1 line mean the model simulates too much of the quantity, points above it too
little. A vegetation class with fewer than 30 burnable cells (2.7\,ha) in a fire's
landscape was dropped from that class's panel: with almost nothing of the class available
to burn, observed and simulated areas are both zero and the residual is only tie
randomization. Simulations that burned fewer than three cells have no principal axis, and
were resampled from the same fire's remaining draws, so the two shape statistics ask a
conditional question --- given that the model produced a fire at all, is the observed
shape a typical one?

\subsubsection*{The regional simulation experiment}

We also simulated fires over the whole study area, partitioned into four latitudinal tiles
that together cover 99.1\,\% of it --- the domain over which the observed record is
complete. For each simulated fire we drew, in this order: an FWI value, resampled with
replacement from the values of the mapped fires, so the simulated marginal matches the
observed one exactly; a draw from the joint posterior of the hyperparameters; a parameter
vector for a \emph{new} fire from the population distribution
$\text{MVLN}(\boldsymbol{\mu}(\mathrm{FWI}), \boldsymbol{\Sigma})$, so that both
hyperparameter and between-fire uncertainty are propagated and no fitted fire's own
parameters are reused; a tile, with probability proportional to the number of eligible
cells it contains; and an ignition cell, uniformly among those. Eligible cells are those
inside the study area, burnable, and far enough from the tile borders that a fire of
$\kappa$ steps cannot reach them --- since the automaton advances at most one cell per
step, this guarantees that no fire is truncated by a tile border. Drawing $\kappa$ before
the tile and the ignition cell, rather than rejecting ignitions that do not fit, leaves the
marginal distribution of $\kappa$ untouched and so does not shrink the simulated size
distribution.

Simulated fires below 10\,ha were discarded, because the mapping records nothing smaller
and they have no observed counterpart. One pass of 148\,649 proposals left 64\,836 fires
of at least 10\,ha (43.6\,\%), and those are the simulated fires analysed below.

\subsubsection*{Analyses}

We compared simulated and observed fires in two ways.

\textbf{Size distribution.} Simulated against observed $\log_{10}(\mathrm{area})$, as
overlaid densities, a quantile--quantile plot and a Kolmogorov--Smirnov statistic.

\textbf{Shape.} From the burned cells of each fire in raster space we computed area,
perimeter, compactness and orientation, the last reported as its deviation from the
dominant wind axis --- $0^\circ$ for a fire running along the wind, $90^\circ$ across it,
and $45^\circ$ the expectation if fires were oriented at random. Here the axis is the
fixed regional direction the tiles were driven with (113/293$^\circ$), because the
observed fires without a mapped ignition point carry no wind field of their own. These
metrics need only a rasterized perimeter, so the observed reference is every mapped fire
in the record across every size class --- 241 perimeters, including the six the model
could not use for lack of weather data --- and not only the 57 with a known ignition
point.

Both shape statistics depend strongly on fire size, and size is itself one of the things
being tested, so the comparisons are made conditionally on burned area as well as
marginally. We repeated them conditionally on FWI, which tests the model's most
distinctive structural claim --- that the parameters of spread shift with fire weather.

\section{Results}

\subsection{What drives spread probability}

Spread probability responded most strongly to the two directional terms. At the mean
FWI of the mapped fires, and holding every other predictor at its mean, spread
probability rose from 0.06 on flat ground to 0.79 towards a neighbour 45$^\circ$ uphill,
and from 0.01 for a neighbour directly upwind to 0.76 for one directly downwind
(Fig.~\ref{fig:curves}). The two flammability indices moved it far less over their
observed ranges: from 0.03 to 0.25 across VFI and from 0.04 to 0.23 across TFI. Both
indices act mostly through their upper half, since the logistic curve is flat where
spread probability is already low; decomposed into the raw predictors they are built
from (Fig.~S2), the strongest single gradient is elevation, with spread probability
falling from 0.25 at 500\,m\,a.s.l.\ to 0.08 at 1200\,m, and northness moves it
hardly at all (0.07 to 0.09 from fully south- to fully north-facing).

Fire weather shifted the curves, but not all of them and not equally. Raising the FWI
anomaly from $-0.60$ to 2.38 --- the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the mapped fires
--- lifted downwind spread probability from 0.59 to 0.88 and nearly doubled spread
probability on flat ground, from 0.055 to 0.100, while leaving the top of the slope
curve unchanged (0.80 to 0.78) and the probability of spreading directly upwind
negligible throughout (0.014 to 0.009) (Fig.~\ref{fig:curves}).

\subsection{Between-fire variation and its relation to fire weather}

The spread parameters varied greatly among fires, and FWI explained only part of that
variation (Fig.~\ref{fig:params}). The number of steps $\kappa$ increased with FWI with
complete certainty (posterior probability of a positive population slope, $P = 1.00$),
and the wind coefficient $\beta_4$ increased too ($P = 0.92$). The other four were
indistinguishable from flat: $P = 0.74$ for the intercept, 0.55 for TFI, 0.34 for slope
and 0.23 for VFI. Fire weather therefore acts mainly on how far a fire runs and on how
strongly wind steers it, and hardly at all on how the local landscape modulates spread.

Across fires the parameters were correlated (Fig.~S3). The strongest association was a
negative one between the VFI and the wind coefficients (posterior mean correlation
$-0.45$): a fire whose spread was strongly wind-driven was one whose spread depended
little on vegetation. The wind coefficient and the number of steps were positively
correlated (0.25) --- windier fires ran further --- and the slope and wind coefficients
negatively ($-0.18$), so that fires tend to be dominated by slope or by wind rather than
by both. Conditioning on FWI left most of these unchanged; the pairs that moved were
those involving $\kappa$ (its correlation with the intercept went from $-0.13$ to
$-0.25$), which is the signature of fire weather acting through the number of steps.

Because the logistic link is nonlinear, a coefficient that does not respond to FWI can
still have a weather-dependent effect on the probability. At the lowest FWI, mean spread
probability ranged from 50\,\% in wet forest through 59\,\% in subalpine forest, 65\,\%
in grassland and 69\,\% in dry forest to 72\,\% in shrubland; at the highest FWI every
type rose, to 71, 79, 83, 86 and 87\,\% respectively (Fig.~\ref{fig:vegeffect}B). The
vegetation effect --- the mean absolute deviation among the five types --- fell from 6.8
to 4.9 percentage points, with posterior probability 0.79 that it declines
(Fig.~\ref{fig:vegeffect}A). Severe fire weather thus narrows the differences among
vegetation types without erasing them, and it narrows them by lifting every type rather
than by holding the flammable ones back.

\subsection{Re-simulating the mapped fires}

Given each fire's own fitted parameters, the simulator reproduced the mapped fires
reasonably well: the median spatial overlap over the 57 fires was 0.53 (range
0.15--0.83; Fig.~S4) and the median ratio of simulated to observed size was 1.09, with
55 of 57 fires within a factor of two (Fig.~S5). Under parameters drawn afresh from the
population distribution, both collapsed --- median overlap 0.11 (range 0.03--0.24) and
median size ratio 1.51, with only 28 of 57 fires within a factor of two. The per-cell
picture is the same (Fig.~\ref{fig:bp}): with fitted parameters the high-probability
region follows the observed perimeter closely, whereas with simulated parameters
probability spreads out around the ignition point and the perimeter is no longer traced.

The quantile residuals sharpen this into a statement about calibration
(Fig.~\ref{fig:dharma}, Table~\ref{tab:dharma}). For burned area, the observed values sat
low in their own predictive distributions under fitted parameters (mean residual 0.373
over all vegetation types, 65\,\% of fires below the simulated median), so the model
burns somewhat too much; under simulated parameters it overshot much further (0.686, only
19\,\% below). Among vegetation classes, grassland was the least calibrated under fitted
parameters (0.231, Kolmogorov--Smirnov $D = 0.52$) and subalpine forest the best (0.534,
$D = 0.22$).

The two shape statistics behaved differently, and they are the paper's main negative
result. Under simulated parameters the mean compactness residual was 0.163, with 96\,\%
of observed fires less compact than the median simulation of their own fire, and the
mean wind-axis residual was 0.245, with 90\,\% of observed fires better aligned with
their own wind than the median simulation. Under fitted parameters both are closer to
calibration (0.429 and 0.325), as expected of a fit diagnostic against an out-of-sample
one, but the sign is the same: real fires are less compact and better aligned with the
wind than the model makes them. Every panel departs from uniformity at $P < 0.05$ in
both modes.

\subsection{The regional simulation}

Over the whole study area, the 64\,836 simulated fires were larger than the mapped
record, and uniformly so rather than in one tail: the quantile--quantile relation sits
below the 1:1 line over the entire range (Fig.~\ref{fig:validation}A), the
Kolmogorov--Smirnov statistic is $D = 0.187$ ($P = 1.1 \times 10^{-7}$), and the offset
is a factor of about two to three through the middle of the distribution --- median 151
against 58\,ha, 75th percentile 904 against 321\,ha, 95th percentile 10\,143 against
3249\,ha. The response of size to fire weather, in contrast, matched: across quartiles of
FWI the observed median area rose from 40 to 278\,ha and the simulated median from 54 to
398\,ha (Fig.~\ref{fig:validation}B), the same relative increase carrying the same
offset.

Shape did not match at any size (Table~\ref{tab:shape}). Simulated fires were rounder
than observed ones in every size class --- median compactness 0.33 against 0.24 below
100\,ha, 0.26 against 0.10 between 100 and 1000\,ha, and 0.09 against 0.03 above 1000\,ha
--- and they were not aligned with the wind. The median deviation of a fire's principal
axis from the regional wind axis was 31, 32 and 20$^\circ$ for observed fires in the three
size classes, against 45$^\circ$ under random orientation, but 54, 58 and 60$^\circ$ for
simulated ones: the observed record is wind-aligned, most strongly among the largest
fires, whereas the simulated set is if anything oriented slightly across the wind.
Conditioning on FWI does not change the picture (Fig.~\ref{fig:validation}B): observed
compactness falls with FWI and simulated compactness falls in parallel, roughly 0.15
higher throughout, and the deviation from the wind axis is flat in both, near 35$^\circ$
observed and 50$^\circ$ simulated.

\subsection*{Fitted and simulated parameters mean different things}

The model reproduces a mapped fire well when it is given that fire's own
parameters and poorly when it is given parameters drawn from the population.
That gap is not a failure of the fit; it is a measurement of how much of a
fire's behaviour is carried by the things the parameters absorb. Within a
fortnight the model sees one wind direction, one regional wind speed, one FWI
value and no suppression at all, so a fire that was fanned by an unusually
strong wind, or stopped early by rain or by fire crews, can only be represented
through its own parameter vector. Simulating with a fresh vector is therefore
simulating a different fire under the same weather index and on the same
landscape, and the drop in overlap from 0.53 to 0.11 measures the width of that
distribution rather than an error. The same reading applies to the size
statistics: under fitted parameters simulated and observed sizes agree within a
factor of two for 55 of the 57 fires, and under simulated parameters they do
not. What the regime simulator needs is the second mode --- it has to generate
plausible fires, not to retrodict particular ones --- which is why the
record-wide experiment, and not the per-fire overlap, is the test that matters
for the model's intended use.

One consequence of the model's structure should be stated plainly rather than
diagnosed away: a simulated fire stops either because propagation failed at
every edge cell or because the step budget $\kappa$ ran out, and the model does
not distinguish the two. $\kappa$ is a lumped stand-in for everything that ends
a fire in time --- a weather change, rain, successful suppression --- and we
make no attempt to attribute individual edges to one mechanism or the other.

\subsection*{The model cannot make an elongated fire}

The clearest mismatch in both validation exercises is shape, and it is
structural rather than a matter of tuning. In the automaton the spread
\emph{rate} is isotropic: the front advances at most one cell per step in each
of the eight directions, and the wind term
$\cos(A_{ij} - D_i)\,V_i\,\beta_4$ changes only \emph{whether} a neighbour
ignites, never how fast the front reaches it. The downwind front therefore
cannot outrun the flanks, which is exactly what makes a real fire a cigar.

Once the wind term is strong enough to matter it drives spread probability
towards 1 downwind and 0 upwind, so downwind and lateral cells both ignite at
nearly every step and the burned set converges on the downwind half of the
square reachable in $\kappa$ steps. The elongation of that half-lobe is fixed by
geometry --- 1.89 for a half-disc and 2.0 for the half-square the eight-neighbour
automaton actually reaches --- and simulations on a flat synthetic landscape
confirm the ceiling: sweeping the base spread probability against $\beta_4$,
elongation never exceeded 1.92 wherever the wind term was active, and beyond
$\beta_4 \approx 4$ every value of the base probability collapsed onto 1.83--1.85,
the wind term having saturated. The
fitted fires sit firmly in that saturated regime: the median fitted wind
coefficient is 4.9, which over typical wind speeds in these landscapes gives a
median downwind-to-upwind logit difference of about 25, and 82\,\% of fires
exceed a difference of 6. Against a ceiling of about 1.9, 61\,\% of the 241
mapped fires are more elongated than the model can be, rising to 78\,\% among
fires larger than 1000\,ha; the observed distribution runs 1.31, 2.12 and 3.78 at
the 10th, 50th and 90th percentiles, with a maximum of 7.55. No value of the
parameters would close that gap.

The one regime in which the automaton does produce cigars is marginal
propagation, where spread probability is low enough that the effective front
speed itself differs between downwind and lateral directions. Forcing it there
--- capping spread probability at 0.5 with a weak base probability --- reached an
elongation of 5.0, but the resulting fire burned 84\,ha. Under this
parameterisation the model can be elongated or large, not both.

We suspect this also explains the size overshoot, and that the two are one
finding rather than two. The step budget itself is not at fault: all 235 fires
inform it, and $\kappa$ drawn from the population reproduces the fitted values
closely (median 40 against 31 steps, 95th percentile 368 against 349). But given
the same reach, a fire that spreads as a round blob burns far more area than one
that spreads as a cigar, so the roundness and the inflated size distribution
point in the same direction, from shape to size. Anything that restored
realistic anisotropy would be expected to pull the simulated size distribution
down with it.

Reproducing fire shape would require a term the model does not have. The two
obvious candidates are rate anisotropy --- letting the front advance more than
one cell per step downwind, as elliptical growth models do
\citep{rothermel_mathematical_1972, finney1998farsite, sullivan2009wildland} ---
or a spread probability capped well below 1 combined with a much larger step
budget, so that the front speed becomes directional through marginal
propagation. Either is a change to the automaton, not a re-estimation of it, and
we did not attempt one here. A second, more prosaic contributor is worth naming:
the wind field is spatially uniform in direction and near-constant in magnitude,
in fitting as in simulation, so the fitted coefficient has no way to express the
day-to-day gustiness that drives real head-fire runs.

\subsection*{Why we did not use classical cross-validation}

Holding out a fifth of the fires would have degraded the model without testing
what matters about it. Fifty-seven fires is already few for a six-parameter
hierarchical model with sign-constrained bounds, a multivariate logit-normal on
the transformed parameters and an auxiliary regression of area on the number of
steps that depends on the fitted $\kappa_f$; the observed size distribution is
heavy-tailed and sharply peaked, which makes it an unstable target to estimate
from a few dozen fires; and the hierarchical structure is itself a regulariser,
so a held-out fire's parameters would mostly reproduce the population mean plus
noise. The model is also deliberately underparameterised for the process ---
fixed functional forms, sign restrictions, a fixed decay $\tau$, bounded
parameter ranges --- so overfitting is not the failure mode cross-validation is
designed to catch. What we want to know is whether the simulator generates fires
with the statistical properties of the ones that actually occur, which is the
question pattern-oriented validation asks
\citep{grimm2005pattern, hartig2011statistical}, and it is the standard by which
burn-probability models are usually judged \citep{parisien2020commentary}.
