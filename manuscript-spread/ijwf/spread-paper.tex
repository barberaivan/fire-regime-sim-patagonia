\subsection{Study area}

The study area covers 29\,214\,km$^2$ of the Subantarctic and High-Andean
phytogeographic provinces of Argentina \citep{oyarzabal_vegetation_2018}, between
39.0$^\circ$ and 44.5$^\circ$\,S: a strip roughly 600\,km from north to south and
50\,km from west to east along the eastern slope of the Andes. It contains  % TODO cite the study-area figure here if one is included
almost all of the continuous forests and shrublands of northwestern Andean Patagonia.
Precipitation falls steeply from west to east, from 3000--4000\,mm\,year$^{-1}$ to
about 500\,mm\,year$^{-1}$ over a few tens of kilometres \citep{villalba_large-scale_2003},
with wet, snowy winters and dry summers. Elevation ranges from 200 to 3700\,m\,a.s.l.,
but burnable vegetation is confined below $\approx$\,1750\,m\,a.s.l., where mean annual
temperature ranges from about 0 to 13\,$^\circ$C. Lakes, glaciers, unvegetated
high-Andean ground and urban areas make up about a quarter of the area and are treated
as non-burnable.

Vegetation follows the precipitation gradient, elevation and local topography.
Wet forests are dominated by the evergreen \textit{Nothofagus dombeyi} (Mirb.) Oerst.
with an understorey of the bamboo \textit{Chusquea culeou} E. Desv.; subalpine forests
of the deciduous \textit{Nothofagus pumilio} (Poepp. \& Endl.) Krasser occupy steep
slopes from $\approx$\,1200\,m\,a.s.l. to treeline; dry forests of the conifer
\textit{Austrocedrus chilensis} (D. Don) Pic.Serm. \& Bizzarri occupy low elevations at
intermediate precipitation; shrublands are formed by resprouting tall shrubs and low
trees such as \textit{Nothofagus antarctica} (G. Forst.) Oerst., \textit{Lomatia
hirsuta} (Lam.) Diels ex J.F. Macbr. and \textit{Schinus patagonicus} (Phil.) I.M.
Johnst. ex Cabrera, often with abundant \textit{C. culeou}; and grasslands correspond
mostly to the Patagonian steppe, with tussock grasses of \textit{Pappostipa} and
\textit{Festuca} and the cushion shrub \textit{Mulinum spinosum} (Cav.) Pers. Fuel
amount and continuity differ sharply among these types: tall forests carry a sparse,
moist understorey vertically separated from the canopy, whereas shrublands have
vertically continuous fuels and a higher proportion of dry fine dead fuel
\citep{paritsis_positive_2015, tiribelli_changes_2018, barbera_microclimate_2023}.

Fires occur in late spring and summer (October--March), when fuels are dry enough, and
are ignited both by lightning and by people \citep{kitzberger_climatic_1997}. The most
fire-prone years are those with warm summers preceded by dry springs
\citep{kitzberger_climatic_1997, veblen_fire_2003}.

The model uses five groups of predictors, all resolved to the 30\,m grid:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Vegetation type}, in five classes (wet forest, subalpine forest, dry
forest, shrubland, grassland), reclassified from the regional forest and land-cover map
of \citet{mapa_ciefap}; areas burned before that map was produced were patched with the
map of \citet{lara_vegetacion_1999}.

\item \textbf{NDVI} (Normalized Difference Vegetation Index), averaged over all
available Landsat images from December to March of the summer preceding each fire
season, as a proxy for productivity and fuel load.

\item \textbf{Elevation, slope and aspect}, derived from the 30\,m SRTM V3 digital
elevation model \citep{DEMfarr2007}. From aspect we computed \textit{northness} as its
cosine, which is 1 on north-facing and $-1$ on south-facing slopes.

\item \textbf{Wind direction and speed}. Lacking meteorological data with adequate
spatial coverage, and with uncertain spread dates for many fires, we assumed that wind
does not change during a fire. This is a coarse approximation, but fires in this region
commonly spread in a single dominant direction. From a regional direction we generated
30\,m direction and speed fields with WindNinja \citep{forthofer2007windNinja}, using
the mass-conservation solver, a 90\,m mesh and a regional speed fixed at
14.4\,km\,h$^{-1}$. The regional direction of each mapped fire was taken from the ERA5
reanalysis \citep{hersbach2020era5} and corrected manually where the fire's shape
disagreed with it. The regional speed is held constant, but the model estimates a
fire-level coefficient that absorbs between-fire variation in wind forcing.

\item \textbf{Atmospheric conditions}, summarized by the Fire Weather Index
(FWI; \citealt{van_wagner_development_1987}), the single weather variable in the model
because it integrates relative humidity, temperature, precipitation and wind speed and
so keeps the number of parameters low. We used the ERA5-based daily FWI of
\citet{vitolo_era5-based_2020} at $\approx$\,24\,km resolution. Because the index varies
strongly along the precipitation gradient while our data cannot inform that variation,
we standardized it per pixel using the mean and standard deviation of the 1998--2023
series, so the predictor represents climatic variation in time and not in space; the
long-term spatial effects of climate are absorbed by the vegetation variables
\citep{barbera_patterns}. Standardized daily values were averaged by fortnight and then
accumulated as a weighted mean over the focal fortnight and the 10 preceding ones, with
weights decaying into the past as $w_l = \exp[-(l/\tau)^2]$. The decay $\tau$ was fixed
at 2.82 fortnights, the joint posterior mode of a model of fire size as a function of
accumulated FWI.
\end{itemize}

Space and time are discrete throughout: the landscape is a rectangular grid of
30\,m\,$\times$\,30\,m cells and the calendar is divided into 14-day fortnights, with
years running from 1 July to 30 June and named after the month in which they end.

\subsubsection*{Flammability indices}

Representing the effects of vegetation type, productivity and topography on
flammability with one coefficient each would have required more parameters than the
data can support. We therefore summarized those predictors into two indices: a
\textbf{vegetation flammability index} (VFI) and a \textbf{topographic flammability
index} (TFI). Rather than an ordination, we used the linear predictor of a logistic
regression of the probability that a cell burned at least once over the study period,
fitted at the regional scale to a sample of 9194 points at least 800\,m apart
\citep{barbera_patterns}, and split it into a vegetation and a topographic part:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:FI}
\begin{aligned}
    \mathrm{VFI} &= \{[a_v + b_v \, (\mathrm{NDVI} - c_v)] + 3.487888\} \ \frac{1}{1.225364} \\
    \mathrm{TFI} &= [(-0.002407865 \, E + 0.2937129 \, N) + 2.691936] \ \frac{1}{0.852505} \\
    N &= \cos \left(\Phi \, \frac{\pi}{180}\right) \frac{1}{1 + \exp(-5 + 35 P)},
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $a_v$, $b_v$ and $c_v$ vary by vegetation type $v$ (Table~1), $E$ is
elevation (m\,a.s.l.), $\Phi$ is aspect (degrees), $P$ is slope (degrees) and $N$ is
northness weighted by slope, so that it approaches zero on flat ground. Both indices are
scaled to mean 0 and standard deviation 1 over the study area. Higher values mean more
flammable vegetation and more flammable topography: VFI is highest in shrublands and dry
forests and lowest in wet forests, and TFI decreases with elevation and increases towards
north-facing slopes.

\subsubsection*{The cellular automaton}

The spread model propagates fire from burning cells to their eight neighbours, with a
structure close to that of \citet{morales_stochastic_2015}. Cells are in one of  % TODO cite the model-schematic figure here if one is included
three states --- burning, burnable or non-burnable --- and a burning cell stays burning
for exactly one iteration (\textit{step} from here on), after which it becomes
non-burnable. Cells may be non-burnable from the start (water, unvegetated ground) or
because they burned earlier in the same year. Simulation starts from at least one
burning cell; at each step, spread from every burning cell to each of its burnable
neighbours is drawn as a Bernoulli trial, and the step ends once all burning cells have
been resolved. A cell with several burning neighbours may be reached by any of them, and
the order in which they are tried is immaterial. The simulation stops when the maximum
number of steps is reached, or earlier if no burning cell manages to spread. Fires are
assumed to occur entirely within one fortnight, so the model is atemporal within that
period.

Spread $y \in \{0, 1\}$ from cell $i$ to a burnable neighbour $j$ at step $k$ is

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:datamodel}
\begin{aligned}
    y_{ijk} &\sim \ \text{Bernoulli}(\theta_{ijk}) \\
    \theta_{ijk} &= \frac{\mathbb{I}_{k \le \kappa}}{1 + \exp{(-\eta_{ij})}} \\
    \eta_{ij} &= \beta_0 + \beta_1 \, \mathrm{VFI}_j + \beta_2 \, \mathrm{TFI}_j
                 + \beta_3 \, S_{ij} + \beta_4 \, W_{ij} \\
    S_{ij} &= \sin\left[ \tan^{-1} \left( \frac{E_j - E_i}{G_{ij}} \right) \right]
              \mathbb{I}_{E_j - E_i > 0} \\
    W_{ij} &= \cos(A_{ij} - D_i) \, V_i,
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $\mathbb{I}_{k \le \kappa}$ indicates whether the number of steps
$\kappa$ has been exceeded. VFI and TFI are properties of the receiving cell, whereas
$S_{ij}$ and $W_{ij}$ are \textit{directional} terms computed from the donor--receiver
pair: $E$ is elevation (m), $G_{ij}$ the distance between cell centres (m), $A_{ij}$ the
direction from which fire would arrive --- the position of the donor as seen from the
receiver (radians) --- $D_i$ the direction the wind blows from at the donor (radians) and
$V_i$ the wind speed at the donor (m\,s$^{-1}$). The first factor of $S_{ij}$ is the sine
of the slope between the two cells, equal to 1 if the receiver lies directly above the
donor and $-1$ if directly below; because cells do not overlap it rarely exceeds
$\sin(45^\circ) = 0.7071$. The indicator $\mathbb{I}_{E_j - E_i > 0}$ switches the term
off for downhill spread. The first factor of $W_{ij}$ is 1 when the wind blows exactly
along the direction of spread, $-1$ when exactly against it and 0 when perpendicular,
and $V_i$ gives the term its magnitude. Over the eight-cell neighbourhood the distances
$G_{ij}$ and directions $A_{ij}$ (in degrees) are

\[
    G =
    \begin{bmatrix}
        30 \sqrt{2} & 30 & 30 \sqrt{2} \\
        30 & \cdot & 30 \\
        30 \sqrt{2} & 30 & 30 \sqrt{2}
    \end{bmatrix}, \quad
    A =
    \begin{bmatrix}
        135 & 180 & 225 \\
        90 & \cdot & 270 \\
        45 & 0 & 315
    \end{bmatrix},
\]

\noindent with $\cdot$ marking the donor. Distance to roads and settlements is assumed
not to affect spread probability. The unknowns are the five coefficients and the number
of steps, collected as
$\boldsymbol{\beta} = \{\beta_0, \beta_1, \beta_2, \beta_3, \beta_4, \kappa\}$.

\subsubsection*{Hierarchical structure}

One of our aims was to model the effect of weather on spread. Weather station density in
the region is far too low to give adequate spatial and temporal resolution over decades,
so we used the coarse, pixel-standardized FWI described above, assumed it constant within
a fire, and took its value at the ignition cell. Even with that variation, the model
remains a simple representation of spread: wind direction and regional speed are constant
within a fire, and suppression is not represented at all. To absorb the process variance
the model does not describe, we gave the parameters themselves a statistical model ---
that is, a hierarchical (multilevel) structure --- so that FWI sets the \emph{mean} of a
distribution of spread parameters rather than determining them. Two fires with the same
FWI can then differ in weather, suppression, soil moisture and terrain particulars:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:hier1}
\begin{aligned}
    \boldsymbol{\beta}_{ft} &= \mathbf{L} + \frac{\mathbf{U} - \mathbf{L}}{1 + \exp{(\boldsymbol{\eta}_{ft}})} \\
    \boldsymbol{\eta}_{ft} &\sim \ \text{Multivariate Normal}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{ft}, \boldsymbol{\Sigma}) \\
    \boldsymbol{\mu}_{ft} &= \boldsymbol{\gamma}_0 + \boldsymbol{\gamma}_1 \, x_{ft} \\
    x_{ft} &= \sum_{l=0}^{10} \mathrm{FWI}_{f,t-l} \ \frac{w_l}{\sum_{j=0}^{10} w_j},
    \qquad w_l = e^{-\left(\frac{l}{\tau}\right)^2},
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $\boldsymbol{\beta}_{ft}$ is the parameter vector of fire $f$, which
occurred in fortnight $t$, and $\mathbf{L}$ and $\mathbf{U}$ hold the lower and upper
bound of each parameter. Bounding the parameters simplified estimation and made sign
restrictions easy to impose, so that $\boldsymbol{\beta}_{ft}$ follows a multivariate
logit-normal distribution scaled between $\mathbf{L}$ and $\mathbf{U}$. The bounds were

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:limits}
\begin{aligned}
    \mathbf{L} &= \{-50, 0, 0, 0, 0, 2\} \\
    \mathbf{U} &= \{50, 30, 30, 158.6232, 30, \psi\}, \qquad
    \psi \sim \ \text{Uniform}(\psi_{\min}, 2000),
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\noindent the upper bound on the number of steps, $\psi$, being estimated. Its prior
upper limit of 2000 steps means a fire can travel at most
$2000 \times 30\,\mathrm{m} = 60\,\mathrm{km}$, which spans the west--east extent of the
Andean Patagonian forests in Argentina; larger fires would leave the study area, and we
have no data to inform spread probability in the steppe. Its lower limit $\psi_{\min}$
was set to the smallest value compatible with the fire-wise posteriors of stage 1
(below). The predictor $x$ was standardized before fitting, and we used
$\gamma_{0p} \sim \text{Normal}(0, 10)$ for $p \in \{1, \ldots, 5\}$,
$\gamma_{06} \sim \text{Normal}(-3.80, 10)$ and
$\boldsymbol{\gamma}_1 \sim \text{Normal}(0, 10)$, where $p$ indexes the six spread
parameters. The covariance matrix was decomposed into standard deviations and a
correlation matrix so that each could take its own prior:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:hier2}
\begin{aligned}
    \boldsymbol{\Sigma} &= \text{diag}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}) \, \boldsymbol{\Omega} \, \text{diag}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}) \\
    \sigma_p^2 &\sim \ \text{Inverse Gamma}(\alpha = 1, \beta = 0.001) \\
    \boldsymbol{\Omega} &= \text{diag}(\boldsymbol{V})^{-\frac{1}{2}} \, \boldsymbol{V} \, \text{diag}(\boldsymbol{V})^{-\frac{1}{2}} \\
    \boldsymbol{V} &\sim \ \text{Inverse Wishart}(\nu = 7, \boldsymbol{\Sigma} = \boldsymbol{I}),
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $\boldsymbol{V}$ is an auxiliary covariance matrix used only to place an
inverse-Wishart prior on the correlation matrix without its parameters constraining the
standard deviations; with $\nu = 7$ the implied prior on each correlation is
approximately uniform on $(-1, 1)$.

Because the parameters have a distribution, a mapped fire can be simulated either with a
parameter vector drawn from that distribution given its FWI, or with the vector estimated
for that particular fire. We call these \textit{simulated} and \textit{fitted} parameters
respectively.

Fitting the spread model is hard because the cell-to-cell spread events $y_{ijk}$ are not
observed. At best the ignition point and the final perimeter of a fire are known; how the
fire moved from cell to cell is not, and could not be recorded, since fire advances
continuously in space and time. Without those data the likelihood cannot be evaluated, so
we used Approximate Bayesian Computation (ABC; \citealt{beaumont2010approximate,
sisson2018handbook}), as \citet{morales_stochastic_2015} did for the same purpose. ABC
applies when a model can simulate data but its likelihood cannot be computed: parameter
values are favoured in proportion to how similar the data they simulate are to the
observed ones. The ABC posterior conditions on a summary of the data rather than on the
data themselves,

\[
p_{\mathrm{ABC}}(\vartheta \mid s_{\text{obs}}) \propto
\int K(||s_{\text{obs}} - s||) \ p(s \mid \vartheta) \ p(\vartheta) \ ds,
\]

\noindent where $s_{\text{obs}}$ summarizes the observed data and $s$ the simulated ones,
$||\cdot||$ is a distance and $K(\cdot)$ a kernel mapping distances to (unnormalized)
densities, larger at smaller distances. Here $y_{\text{obs}}$ would be the unobserved
binary vector of spread events and $s_{\text{obs}}$ is the set of cells the fire burned.
Our dissimilarity is $||s_i - s_j|| = 1 - O_{ij}$, with $O_{ij}$ the spatial overlap
between the observed and a simulated fire,

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:overlap}
O_{ij} = \frac{|A_i \cap A_j|}{|A_i \cup A_j|},
\end{equation}

\noindent where $A$ is the set of cells burned by each fire, so that $O_{ij} = 1$ for
identical fires and 0 when they share no burned cell. Every simulation of a mapped fire
uses that fire's own data: its spatial predictors, its FWI and its ignition point.

The full hierarchical model could not be sampled directly: the parameters are many, the
kernel is stochastic, and it varies abruptly with $\boldsymbol{\beta}$, which makes
conventional MCMC prohibitively expensive. We therefore fitted it in two stages, following
\citet{lunn2013fully}: stage 1 samples the posterior of $\boldsymbol{\beta}_f$ separately
for each fire under a surrogate prior, and stage 2 combines those samples to estimate the
hyperparameters, discounting the surrogate prior in the process. Splitting the model this
way lets the difficult step --- sampling $\boldsymbol{\beta}_f$ --- be handled with
whatever algorithm suits it.

\subsubsection*{Stage 1 --- fire-wise posteriors}

For each of the 57 fires with a known ignition point we sampled $\boldsymbol{\beta}_f$
with adaptive ABC sequential Monte Carlo \citep{delmoral2012adaptive}. The surrogate prior
is uniform over the bounded parameter space $[\mathbf{L}, \mathbf{U}]$, which is a
standard logistic distribution on the unconstrained scale on which the sampler works. We
used $N = 10\,000$ particles, initialized from a Sobol sequence, and one simulated fire
per particle. At each iteration the tolerance $\varepsilon$ was lowered by bisection to
the value that halves the effective sample size, particles were reweighted under the
kernel

\[
K(d) = (1 - d) \ \mathbb{I}_{d \le \varepsilon},
\]

\noindent that is, the spatial overlap truncated at the current tolerance, and then moved
by one Metropolis step with a differential-evolution proposal
\citep{terbraak2006markov}: the proposal for a particle is its current position plus the
scaled difference between two other particles drawn with probability proportional to
their weights, plus a small Gaussian jitter (scale $10^{-3}$). Sampling stopped when the
acceptance rate fell below 0.015, when fewer than 300 distinct particles survived a move,
or when $\varepsilon$ stopped decreasing appreciably (less than $10^{-4}$ for ten
consecutive iterations). The upper bound on $\kappa$ was set per fire to the value that
allows simulating fires large enough to reach a very low overlap with the observed one,
which bounds the search without excluding plausible sizes.

\subsubsection*{Stage 2 --- hierarchical fit}

Stage 2 ran an MCMC over all parameters, using the stage-1 samples as the proposal
distribution for each $\boldsymbol{\beta}_f$, discounting the stage-1 surrogate prior, and
updating most hyperparameters by Gibbs steps \citep{lunn2013fully}.

The 57 fires with a known ignition point are larger, on average, than the mapped record as
a whole, which would bias the parameters that govern simulated fire size --- the number of
steps $\kappa_f$ and its hyperparameters $\gamma_{06}$, $\gamma_{16}$ and $\sigma_6$. To
correct that bias we fitted, jointly with the rest of the model, an auxiliary regression
relating the estimated number of steps to fire size, which lets the steps of the 178 fires
with unknown ignition point --- which cannot be simulated --- be inferred from their area:

\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    \log(a_f) &\sim \ \text{Normal}(\mu_f, \sigma_a)\text{T}[\log(10), ] \\
    \mu_f &= \alpha_0 + \alpha_1 \, \log(\kappa_f), \qquad
    \alpha_0 \sim \ \text{Normal}(-1.52, 10), \quad
    \alpha_1 \sim \ \text{Normal}(0, 10),
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\noindent with $a_f$ the area of fire $f$ (ha), truncated below at the 10\,ha mapping
threshold. The prior on $\alpha_0$ was centred on the mean of $\log(a)$ over all mapped
fires so as not to bias its estimation.

The fire simulator is written in C\texttt{++} and distributed as the R package
\texttt{FireSpread} \citep{firespread} through \texttt{Rcpp} \citep{Rcpp}. All fitting and
analysis was done in R 4.2.0 \citep{R}, with \texttt{terra} \citep{terra} for spatial data.

Classical train/test validation is not appropriate here, for reasons we return to in the
Discussion, and burn-probability models of this kind are conventionally judged on the
emergent properties of the regime they generate rather than on point predictions of
individual fires \citep{parisien2020commentary}. We therefore validated the model in the
pattern-oriented sense \citep{grimm2005pattern, hartig2011statistical}: we asked whether
statistical patterns emerging from many simulated fires match those of the observed
record, using patterns the fitting procedure did not target.

\subsubsection*{The simulation experiment}

We simulated fires over the whole study area, partitioned into four latitudinal tiles that
together cover 99.1\,\% of it --- the domain over which the observed record is complete.
For each simulated fire we drew, in this order: an FWI value, resampled with replacement
from the values of the mapped fires, so the simulated marginal matches the observed one
exactly; a draw from the joint posterior of the hyperparameters; a parameter vector for a
\emph{new} fire from the population distribution
$\text{MVLN}(\boldsymbol{\mu}(\mathrm{FWI}), \boldsymbol{\Sigma})$, so that both
hyperparameter and between-fire uncertainty are propagated and no fitted fire's own
parameters are reused; a tile, with probability proportional to the number of eligible
cells it contains; and an ignition cell, uniformly among those. Eligible cells are those
inside the study area, burnable, and far enough from the tile borders that a fire of
$\kappa$ steps cannot reach them --- since the automaton advances at most one cell per
step, this guarantees that no fire is truncated by a tile border. Drawing $\kappa$ before
the tile and the ignition cell, rather than rejecting ignitions that do not fit, leaves the
marginal distribution of $\kappa$ untouched and so does not shrink the simulated size
distribution.

Simulated fires below 10\,ha were discarded, because the mapping records nothing smaller
and they have no observed counterpart; about 56\,\% of proposals fall below the threshold,
and the run proceeded in passes until 64\,836 fires of at least 10\,ha had been accepted.
No recalibration of any parameter was applied.

\subsubsection*{Analyses}

We compared simulated and observed fires in three ways, all of them conditioned on fire
size where the statistic depends on it.

\textbf{Size distribution.} Simulated against observed $\log_{10}(\mathrm{area})$, as a
quantile--quantile plot and a Kolmogorov--Smirnov statistic.

\textbf{Shape.} From the burned cells of each fire in raster space we computed area,
perimeter, compactness ($4\pi \times$ area / perimeter$^2$), the convex-hull fill ratio,
and --- by principal component analysis of the coordinates of the burned cells ---
elongation, as the ratio of the principal axes, and orientation, as the bearing of the
leading eigenvector. Orientation is compared with the dominant wind axis, so that
wind-driven fires should run along it. These metrics need only a rasterized perimeter, so
the observed reference is every mapped fire in every size class, not only the 57 with a
known ignition point. For simulated fires, where the wind field is known, we additionally
measured elongation \emph{along the wind axis} --- spread along it divided by spread across
it --- taking the axis as the circular mean of wind direction over the cells the fire
burned, since WindNinja steers the field by terrain.

\textbf{Spatial signature.} For each fire we summarized how the spatial predictors relate
to burning at its edges, and compared the distribution of those summaries across fires.
For every burned cell with at least one burnable unburned neighbour we formed a stratum
whose members are that cell's burnable neighbours and whose response is whether each
burned; this is exactly the set of Bernoulli trials the simulator itself performs. We then
fitted a conditional logistic regression per fire \citep{survival}, with VFI and TFI as
predictors in a single multiple regression. Holding the donor cell fixed conditions out
the fire, the weather, suppression and the donor itself, and the intercept drops out of the
conditional likelihood, so only contrasts between neighbouring cells are compared. The
slope and wind terms of Eqn~\ref{eq:datamodel} are deliberately excluded: they are
directional, and for an observed fire the order in which cells burned is unknown, so the
donor is a guess at the arrival direction rather than a measurement. They are tested
through fire shape instead. Donors were subsampled to 1500 per fire, and the identical
procedure was applied to simulated and to observed fires.

Finally, we repeated the shape and signature comparisons within quartiles of FWI, which
tests the model's most distinctive structural claim --- that the spatial coefficients of
spread shift with fire weather.
